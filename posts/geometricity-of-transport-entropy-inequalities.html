Which transport–entropy inequalities are geometric?

2026-09-21 · optimal transport, information theory

Talagrand's transport inequality says that for the standard Gaussian \(\gamma\) on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the cost of transporting \(\gamma\) to \(\mu\) is controlled by how far \(\mu\) is from Gaussian in relative entropy: \(W_2^2(\mu,\gamma) \le 2D(\mu \Vert \gamma)\). Inequalities of this shape — entropy on one side, transport cost on the other — show up all over probability and convex geometry, and the interesting question is almost never whether such an inequality holds. It is what the best constant is, and whether Gaussians achieve it.

Courtade and Wang's recent generalized Blaschke–Santaló-type inequalities give a large family of these at once, indexed by a choice of weights and a quadratic cost. Each member comes with a sharp additive constant. For most members that constant is some unpleasant number. For a few it is exactly zero, with standard Gaussians extremal — and those are the ones with genuine geometric content. This post is about telling the two apart.

The punchline is that membership in the good class is decided by three conditions, two of which are easy linear-algebra checks and one of which is a genuinely delicate \(\log\det\) inequality. I will derive all three, show why the third is the hard one, and then prove that the Wasserstein barycenter satisfies it — by a computation in which a single Gaussian random variable does all the work.

The setup

Fix Euclidean spaces \(E_i = \mathbb{R}^{d_i}\) and let \(E = \bigoplus_i E_i\). A datum is a pair \((c,Q)\): weights \(c_i \gt 0\) and a symmetric operator \(Q\) on \(E\). Package them into a single operator

\[ \Theta \;:=\; \mathrm{diag}(c_1 \mathrm{id}_{E_1}, \ldots, c_n \mathrm{id}_{E_n}) - 2Q, \qquad \langle x, \Theta x\rangle \;=\; \sum_i c_i |x_i|^2 - 2\langle x, Qx \rangle . \]

For centered marginals \(\mu_i\) on \(E_i\), the inequality under study is

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n c_i\, D(\mu_i \Vert \gamma_{E_i}) \;\ge\; \frac12 \inf_{\mu \in \Pi(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)} \int_E \langle x, \Theta x\rangle \, d\mu \;+\; \widetilde{D}, \]

where \(\Pi\) is the set of couplings and \(\widetilde{D}\) is the sharp additive constant — the largest number making this true for all marginals. The datum is called geometric when \(\widetilde{D} = 0\) and standard Gaussians are extremal. That is the trivial constant, and it is the case where the inequality says something clean.

The first real simplification is that we may restrict to Gaussian marginals. Courtade and Wang prove an unconditional Gaussian-saturation theorem for exactly this deficit: the inequality is hardest in the Gaussian regime. So write \(\mu_i = N(0,\Sigma_i)\) and set

\[ E(\Sigma) := \sum_i c_i D\big(N(0,\Sigma_i) \Vert \gamma_{E_i}\big), \qquad V(\Sigma) := \tfrac12 \min_{S \in \Pi(\Sigma_1,\ldots,\Sigma_n)} \mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S), \qquad \Phi := E - V. \]

Here \(E\) is the entropy side, \(V\) the transport side, and \(\Phi\) the deficit. Gaussian saturation says \(\widetilde{D} = \inf_\Sigma \Phi(\Sigma)\), so

Geometricity, restated The datum is geometric exactly when \(\inf_\Sigma \Phi(\Sigma) = 0\), attained at \(\Sigma = \mathrm{id}\).

Note that \(\Phi = E - V\) is a difference of two convex functions, so it is neither convex nor concave. That is the source of every difficulty below.

The characterization

Theorem The datum \((c,Q)\) is geometric if and only if
  • (G1) \(\Theta \succeq 0\);
  • (G2) there is a witness \(K \in \Pi(\mathrm{id},\ldots,\mathrm{id})\) — that is, \(K \succeq 0\) with \(K_{ii} = \mathrm{id}_{E_i}\) — satisfying \(\Theta K = 0\);
  • (G3) for every block-diagonal \(\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\Lambda_1,\ldots,\Lambda_n) \preceq \Theta\), \[ \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \log\det\Big( \mathrm{id}_{E_i} - \tfrac{1}{c_i}\Lambda_i \Big) \;\ge\; 0. \tag{$\star$} \]
Under (G1)–(G2), the Gaussian coupling \(N(0,K)\) is optimal for the standard Gaussian marginals.

Read in order, the three conditions say: the cost is nonnegative; standard Gaussians are extremal; and standard Gaussians are globally optimal, not merely critical. The first two are witness conditions you can check by hand. The third is the real content.

Where (G1) comes from

This one is nearly immediate. If \(\Theta \succeq 0\) then \(\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S) \ge 0\) for every coupling \(S \succeq 0\), so \(V \ge 0\). Since \(E(\mathrm{id}) = 0\) — a standard Gaussian has zero relative entropy to itself — we get \(\Phi(\mathrm{id}) = -V(\mathrm{id}) \le 0\), and hence \(\widetilde{D} \le 0\). Geometricity is then the one-sided statement \(\inf_\Sigma \Phi \ge 0\).

If instead \(\Theta \not\succeq 0\), the cost is indefinite, \(\widetilde{D}\) can be strictly positive, and the problem leaves this setting altogether. So (G1) is the admissibility condition.

Where (G2) comes from

We need \(\Phi(\mathrm{id}) = 0\), and we just observed \(E(\mathrm{id}) = 0\). So we need \(V(\mathrm{id}) = 0\): the cheapest coupling of standard Gaussians must have zero cost. Given (G1), for \(S \succeq 0\),

\[ \mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S) \;=\; \mathrm{Tr}\big(\Theta^{1/2} S \,\Theta^{1/2}\big) \;=\; \big\Vert \Theta^{1/2} S^{1/2} \big\Vert_{HS}^2 , \]

so \(\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S) = 0\) if and only if \(\Theta S = 0\). Therefore \(V(\mathrm{id}) = 0\) is precisely the existence of a coupling \(K\) of the standard Gaussians — \(K \succeq 0\), \(K_{ii} = \mathrm{id}\) — that is annihilated by \(\Theta\). That is (G2), and the witness \(K\) is not just a certificate but the optimal coupling itself.

Two immediate consequences. First, (G2) forces \(\Theta\) to be singular: it has \(K\) in its kernel. Geometric data live on the boundary of the PSD cone, never in its interior. Second, (G1) and (G2) together only pin down the behaviour at \(\Sigma = \mathrm{id}\). They say the origin is a critical point. They say nothing about whether it is a global minimum — and, as we will see, they genuinely do not imply it.

Deriving (G3)

Condition (G3) is the global-validity half, and it appears only after the transport value \(V\) has been dualized. The derivation is four steps: reduce to Gaussians, reduce couplings to covariances, dualize the resulting semidefinite program, then minimize in closed form.

Step 1: couplings reduce to covariances

For \(X \sim N(0,S)\) we have \(\mathbb{E}\langle X, \Theta X\rangle = \mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta X X^\top) = \mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S)\), and the \(i\)-th diagonal block of \(S\) is the marginal covariance. Conversely any \(S \succeq 0\) with the right diagonal blocks is the covariance of a Gaussian coupling. So with \(\Pi(\Sigma_1,\ldots,\Sigma_n) := \{ S \succeq 0 : \pi_i S \pi_i^* = \Sigma_i \}\), the transport value is a semidefinite program:

\[ V(\Sigma) = \tfrac12 \min\big\{ \mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S) : S \in \Pi(\Sigma_1,\ldots,\Sigma_n) \big\}. \]

The minimum is attained: \(\Pi(\Sigma)\) is closed, and \(0 \preceq S\) forces \(\Vert S \Vert \le \mathrm{Tr}\,S = \sum_i \mathrm{Tr}\,\Sigma_i\), so it is compact.

Step 2: dualize

Attach a symmetric multiplier \(\Lambda_i\) to each block constraint \(\pi_i S \pi_i^* = \Sigma_i\) and assemble \(\Lambda := \mathrm{diag}(\Lambda_1,\ldots,\Lambda_n)\). Since \(\sum_i \mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda_i \pi_i S \pi_i^*) = \mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda S)\), the Lagrangian collapses to

\[ L(S,\Lambda) \;=\; \mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S) - \sum_i \mathrm{Tr}\big(\Lambda_i(\pi_i S \pi_i^* - \Sigma_i)\big) \;=\; \mathrm{Tr}\big((\Theta - \Lambda)S\big) + \langle \Lambda, \Sigma\rangle , \]

and \(\inf_{S \succeq 0} \mathrm{Tr}((\Theta-\Lambda)S)\) is \(0\) when \(\Theta - \Lambda \succeq 0\) and \(-\infty\) otherwise. Weak duality is then a single line: whenever \(S \succeq 0\) and \(\Lambda \preceq \Theta\),

\[ \mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S) - \langle \Lambda, \Sigma \rangle = \mathrm{Tr}\big((\Theta - \Lambda)S\big) \ge 0 . \]

For strong duality, the independent coupling \(S_0 = \mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_1,\ldots,\Sigma_n) \succ 0\) is strictly primal-feasible, so Slater's condition closes the gap. Writing \(\mathcal{F} := \{ \Lambda \text{ block-diagonal}, \Lambda \preceq \Theta \}\),

\[ V(\Sigma) \;=\; \tfrac12 \max_{\Lambda \in \mathcal{F}} \langle \Lambda, \Sigma \rangle . \]

This is the step that turns (G3) into an operator condition on \(\Theta\) rather than an unmanageable family of scalar tests.

Step 3: exchange the infima

Because \(V\) is now a maximum, \(-V\) is a minimum, and the deficit becomes a joint infimum:

\[ \Phi(\Sigma) = E(\Sigma) - \tfrac12 \max_{\Lambda \in \mathcal{F}} \langle \Lambda, \Sigma\rangle = \inf_{\Lambda \in \mathcal{F}} \Big[ E(\Sigma) - \tfrac12 \langle \Lambda, \Sigma \rangle \Big] , \]

so the two infima may be exchanged freely:

\[ \inf_\Sigma \Phi \;=\; \inf_{\Lambda \in \mathcal{F}} g(\Lambda), \qquad g(\Lambda) := \inf_\Sigma \Big[ E(\Sigma) - \tfrac12\langle \Lambda, \Sigma\rangle \Big]. \tag{1} \]

I will call (1) the flip. It is the hinge of the whole theory, and it gets used in both directions.

Step 4: minimize in closed form

Since \(\langle \Lambda, \Sigma\rangle = \sum_i \mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda_i \Sigma_i)\) sees only the diagonal blocks, \(g\) separates over \(i\). Using \(D(N(0,\Sigma_i)\Vert \gamma) = \tfrac12(\mathrm{Tr}\,\Sigma_i - d_i - \log\det\Sigma_i)\), the \(i\)-th term is

\[ \inf_{\Sigma_i \succ 0} \frac{c_i}{2}\Big[ \mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_i T_i) - \log\det\Sigma_i - d_i \Big], \qquad T_i := \mathrm{id}_{E_i} - \tfrac{1}{c_i}\Lambda_i . \]

Two cases, and both are instructive:

  • If \(T_i\) has an eigenvalue \(\le 0\), with unit eigenvector \(v\), run \(\Sigma_i = \mathrm{id} + t vv^*\). The trace term stays bounded while \(\log\det\Sigma_i = \log(1+t) \to \infty\), so the block infimum is \(-\infty\).
  • If \(T_i \succ 0\), the integrand is strictly convex with gradient \(\tfrac{c_i}{2}(T_i - \Sigma_i^{-1})\), vanishing uniquely at \(\Sigma_i = T_i^{-1}\). There \(\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_i T_i) = d_i\) cancels the constant, leaving \(\tfrac{c_i}{2}\log\det T_i\).

Summing over \(i\) gives the clean formula

\[ g(\Lambda) \;=\; \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \log\det\Big(\mathrm{id}_{E_i} - \tfrac{1}{c_i}\Lambda_i\Big), \]

and combining with the flip (1), geometricity \(\inf_\Sigma \Phi \ge 0\) holds if and only if \((\star)\) holds for every block-diagonal \(\Lambda \preceq \Theta\). That is (G3).

Why (G3) is the hard one

Look at what \((\star)\) actually asks. Since \(\log\det\) is concave and \(\Lambda_i \mapsto T_i\) is affine, \(g\) is concave on the convex set \(\mathcal{F}\). Minimizing a concave function over a convex set is governed entirely by the boundary — never by an interior stationary point. And as any \(T_i\) approaches the boundary of the positive cone, \(g \to -\infty\).

So a priori the infimum may be \(-\infty\): a non-geometric datum can violate \((\star)\) by an unbounded margin. This is not a hypothetical.

(G1) and (G2) do not imply (G3) Take \(n=3\), \(d=1\), and \[ \Theta = \tfrac14 ww^\top, \quad w = (2,-1,-1), \quad c = \big(1,\tfrac14,\tfrac14\big), \quad K = \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top . \] Since \(w^\top \mathbf{1} = 0\), \(\Theta\) is rank one and PSD, and \(\Theta K = 0\) — so (G1) and (G2) both hold. But along the feasible boundary family \(\Lambda(m) = \mathrm{diag}\big(\tfrac{2m}{1+2m}, -m, -m\big)\), the value of \((\star)\) is \[ \log\frac{\sqrt{1+4m}}{1+2m} \;\xrightarrow[\;m\to\infty\;]{}\; -\infty . \] So \(\widetilde{D} = -\infty\): no finite constant makes the inequality hold.

This is why the two easy conditions are not the answer. Criticality at \(\Sigma = \mathrm{id}\) is cheap; global validity is not.

The practical consequence is that one should almost never attack the boundary minimization directly. Instead, use the flip (1) in reverse: exhibit \(\Phi \ge 0\) on the primal side, and the dual statement \((\star)\) follows for free. The data for which this is tractable are exactly the ones whose structure pins down the minimizer — and the cleanest such case is the barycenter.

The Wasserstein barycenter is geometric

Fix weights \(\lambda_i \gt 0\) with \(\sum_i \lambda_i = 1\), and take

\[ c_i = \lambda_i(1-\lambda_i), \qquad \Theta = M \otimes \mathrm{id}_d, \qquad M = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda) - \lambda\lambda^\top, \tag{2} \]

so that \(\langle x, \Theta x\rangle = \sum_{i \lt j} \lambda_i\lambda_j |x_i - x_j|^2\), the barycenter cost. Everything tensors with \(\mathrm{id}_d\), so take \(d=1\). Write \(g(\sigma) := \sigma^2 - 2\log\sigma - 1\) and \(\varphi(t) := t - \log t - 1\); both are \(\ge 0\), vanishing only at argument \(1\), and \(D(N(0,\sigma^2)\Vert\gamma) = \tfrac12 g(\sigma)\).

(G1) is a variance

For any \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\),

\[ x^\top M x \;=\; \sum_i \lambda_i x_i^2 - \Big(\sum_i \lambda_i x_i\Big)^{\!2} \;=\; \mathrm{Var}_\lambda(x) \;\ge\; 0 . \]

So \(M \succeq 0\) and \(\Theta \succeq 0\). Moreover \(M\mathbf{1} = \lambda - \lambda(\lambda^\top \mathbf{1}) = 0\), so \(M\) has rank \(n-1\) with \(\ker M = \mathrm{span}(\mathbf{1})\). That kernel is about to do all the work.

(G2) forces the comonotone coupling

A witness needs \(K \succeq 0\), \(K_{ii} = 1\), and \(MK = 0\). The last condition forces every column of \(K\) into \(\ker M = \mathrm{span}(\mathbf{1})\). A symmetric PSD matrix all of whose columns are parallel to \(\mathbf{1}\) must be \(K = t\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\), and \(K_{ii} = 1\) gives \(t = 1\). So the witness is unique:

\[ K = \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top, \]

which is the covariance of the comonotone coupling \((X, X, \ldots, X)\) with \(X \sim N(0,1)\) — every marginal is the same random variable. By the Theorem, this is the optimal coupling of the standard Gaussians.

(G3) by a pairwise certificate

Now the flip. Rather than face the concave minimization, exhibit \(\Phi \ge 0\) directly. Two structural facts collapse it into nonnegative pairs.

The energy splits over pairs. Because \(\sum_j \lambda_j = 1\), the weights satisfy \(c_i = \lambda_i(1-\lambda_i) = \sum_{j \ne i}\lambda_i\lambda_j\), so

\[ E(\sigma) = \frac12 \sum_i c_i\, g(\sigma_i) = \frac12 \sum_{i \lt j} \lambda_i\lambda_j\big( g(\sigma_i) + g(\sigma_j)\big) . \]

The transport value is computed by one Gaussian. For \(X \sim N(0,S)\) with \(\mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \sigma_i^2\), at a fixed coupling

\[ \tfrac12 \mathrm{Tr}(\Theta S) = \tfrac12\, \mathbb{E} \sum_{i \lt j}\lambda_i\lambda_j (X_i - X_j)^2 = \frac12\Big[ \sum_i \lambda_i \sigma_i^2 - \mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_i \lambda_i X_i\Big)\Big] . \]

Minimizing over couplings therefore maximizes \(\mathrm{Var}(\sum_i \lambda_i X_i)\). That variance is at most \((\sum_i \lambda_i \sigma_i)^2\) by Cauchy–Schwarz, and equality holds exactly when the \(X_i\) are all positive multiples of a single random variable — the comonotone coupling \(X_i = \sigma_i Z\), \(Z \sim N(0,1)\). Hence

\[ V(\sigma) = \tfrac12 \mathrm{Var}_\lambda(\sigma) = \frac12 \sum_{i \lt j}\lambda_i\lambda_j(\sigma_i - \sigma_j)^2 . \]

This is the stochastic step: the entire semidefinite program is solved by writing every marginal as a scaling of one Gaussian.

Subtract. Both \(E\) and \(V\) are \(\tfrac12\sum_{i \lt j}\lambda_i\lambda_j(\cdots)\) with the same nonnegative weights, so it is enough to look at a single pair, and the bracket telescopes:

\[ g(\sigma_i) + g(\sigma_j) - (\sigma_i - \sigma_j)^2 = 2\big( \sigma_i\sigma_j - \log(\sigma_i\sigma_j) - 1 \big) = 2\varphi(\sigma_i\sigma_j) . \]

Both squares cancel and only the product survives. Therefore

The certificate \[ \Phi(\sigma) \;=\; \sum_{i \lt j} \lambda_i \lambda_j\, \varphi(\sigma_i\sigma_j) \;\ge\; 0, \] with equality exactly at \(\sigma = \mathbf{1}\). So \((\star)\) holds and the barycenter datum is geometric.

Notice there is no \(-\infty\) mode here, and the reason is visible in the formula: in \(\varphi(t) = t - \log t - 1\) the runaway \(-\log t\) is always outrun by the linear \(+t\), so no marginal escaping to \(0\) or \(\infty\) can drive \(\Phi\) negative. Compare the counterexample above, where the off-diagonal sign was positive and the comonotone witness could not telescope the deficit into pairs.

Where the log-det minimization actually lands

The flip also locates the boundary minimizer of the concave problem, and the answer is satisfying: it is \(\Lambda = 0\). There every \(T_i = \mathrm{id}\), so the dual value is \(\tfrac12\sum_i c_i \log\det\mathrm{id} = 0\). This \(\Lambda = 0\) sits on \(\partial\mathcal{F}\) — consistent with the boundary principle — precisely because \(\Theta\) is singular: \(\Theta\mathbf{1} = 0\) makes the constraint \(\Lambda \preceq \Theta\) tight along \(\mathbf{1}\), so the minimizer is forced to the boundary rather than escaping to \(-\infty\).

And that same degeneracy \(\Theta\mathbf{1} = 0\) is exactly what supplied the (G2) witness \(K = \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\), whose columns span \(\ker M\). The fact that pins \((\star)\) at zero and the fact that furnishes the witness are one and the same.

Why the barycenter was tractable

The pairwise certificate is clean but looks like a lucky algebraic accident. It is not. There is a change of variables that explains it.

Put \(u_i := c_i - \Lambda_i\) and \(N := \mathrm{diag}(c) - \Theta\), and let \(\mathcal{S} := \{ u \in \mathbb{R}^n_{\gt 0} : \mathrm{diag}(u) \succeq N \}\). Then \(\Lambda \mapsto \mathrm{diag}(u)\) is a bijection from \(\mathcal{F}\) onto \(\mathcal{S}\), carrying \(\Lambda = 0\) to \(u = c\), and

\[ g(\Lambda) = \tfrac12\big(\ell(u) - \ell(c)\big), \qquad \ell(u) = \sum_i c_i \log u_i . \]

Now recall that the positive-definite matrices form a unique-geodesic metric space under \(A \#_t B = A^{1/2}(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2})^t A^{1/2}\), and that the diagonal slice is totally geodesic with \(x = \log u\) straightening geodesics into segments. In that chart \(\ell\) is geodesically affine, and one can show \(\mathcal{S}\) is geodesically convex. So \((\star)\) is a linear functional minimized over a convex region — a geodesically linear program. The concavity that made (G3) frightening was an artifact of the \(\Lambda\)-coordinates.

In such a program first-order optimality implies global optimality, and \(u = c\) is first-order optimal. That gives a second, certificate-free proof that the barycenter is geometric.

There is a pleasant surprise hiding here too. In the chart \(x = \log u\), with \(y_i := x_i - \log \lambda_i^2\), the feasible region is

\[ \mathcal{T}_n = \Big\{ y \in \mathbb{R}^n : \sum_i \frac{1}{1+e^{y_i}} \le 1 \Big\}, \]

the same body for every choice of weights — the weights enter only through a translation. For \(n=2\) this is a half-space, so the content begins at \(n \ge 3\), where the boundary is genuinely curved and the summands are not individually convex.

Beyond the barycenter

Two things generalize. First, geodesic convexity of \(\mathcal{S}\) follows from a clean operator criterion — roughly, that \(\mathrm{diag}(u) \succeq N\) should force \(\mathrm{diag}(u) \succeq -N\) — which can be checked combinatorially. Build the graph on \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\) with an edge wherever \(N_{ij} \ne 0\). If the signed graph is balanced, or if the graph is bipartite, the criterion holds; both are decidable in \(O(n^2)\) by breadth-first two-colouring, and the test localizes to 2-connected blocks. For the barycenter, \(N_{ij} = \lambda_i\lambda_j \gt 0\) off the diagonal, so \(N\) is entrywise nonnegative, hence balanced — a two-line proof of the convexity that otherwise took an epigraph induction.

Second, non-geometric data are not all alike. Tracking the gap \(D_g := -\widetilde{D} \in [0,\infty]\), every datum satisfying (G1) is geometric (\(D_g = 0\)), finite-gap (\(0 \lt D_g \lt \infty\)), or infinite-gap (\(D_g = \infty\)). A single scalar sweeps all three. Take \(n=2\), \(d=1\), \(c=(1,1)\) and

\[ \Theta_q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -q \\ -q & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad 0 \lt q \le 1 . \]

Writing \(p := \sigma_1\sigma_2\), the quadratic terms cancel and \(\Phi = qp - \log p - 1\), minimized at \(p = 1/q\), so \(\widetilde{D} = \log q\). At \(q=1\) this is the symmetrized Talagrand cost \(|x_1-x_2|^2\): \(\Theta_1\) is singular, (G2) holds with witness \(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\), and the datum is geometric. For \(0 \lt q \lt 1\), \(\Theta_q \succ 0\) is nonsingular, so no witness exists, (G2) fails, and the gap is finite. As \(q \to 0^+\) it diverges. For \(q \gt 1\), (G1) fails outright.

So geometricity is a knife-edge: it happens exactly where \(\Theta\) touches the boundary of the PSD cone. Push inward and you overshoot into a finite gap; push outward and the cost goes indefinite.

What I want next

The barycenter proof above has a shape worth taking seriously. The semidefinite program was not solved by semidefinite programming — it was solved by writing \(X_i = \sigma_i Z\) for a single Gaussian \(Z\) and reading off a variance. The dual, the \(\log\det\), and the boundary analysis all became unnecessary the moment the right coupling was guessed.

The open question I am working on is how far that goes: identify the geometric instances of the Courtade–Wang family in general, and give stochastic proofs of optimality rather than convex-analytic ones. The barycenter admits a stochastic-plus-induction argument, and it is a plausible prototype for a broader class. The combinatorial criteria above are suggestive here — balance and bipartiteness are statements about sign patterns in a graph, and sign patterns are exactly what decide whether a comonotone-type coupling can telescope a deficit into nonnegative pieces.

If that correspondence is real, the right statement is probably not about \(\log\det\) at all.
